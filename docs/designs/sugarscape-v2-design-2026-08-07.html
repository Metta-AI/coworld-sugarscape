Sugarscape v2 — Design

The settled design for our own Sugarscape: player agency built into every mechanic as an explicit gate, over one all-integer deterministic core that runs identically as Arena WASM components, a WebSocket container, and a browser replay resimulator. For James (author/reviewer) and implementing agents.

Executive Summary

Sugarscape v2 is our own Sugarscape — not a port of the DTL Python model (platform decision D1 in docs/what-is-a-coworld.md). The founding move is a clean split the classic model never made: Epstein & Axtell hard-code agent behavior — the best-cell movement rule, MRS-crossing trade, automatic mating, the combat retaliation look-ahead — directly into the simulation. v2 evicts all of it to submitted player policies and keeps only physics. Every classic behavioral rule became either a policy gate (a named point in the timestep where each agent's policy is consulted) or a negotiation protocol between agents. That is precisely the structure DTL lacked and the retired v1 could not retrofit: v1 exposed exactly one gate, movement, because its closed 14-step agent turn allowed nothing else archived/v1/.

All 35 open design questions (A1–F6) were settled on 2026-08-07; this document is the record, rendered from docs/v2-design.md. The most consequential calls: scoring is time-integrated Cobb–Douglas welfare, summed across a seat's agents and divided by episode length (B2+B3+B5) — the one scheme under which survival, prosperity, and reproduction all point the same way; the timestep is phased — world physics, then five action gates, then upkeep (E8); and determinism is total — no floats anywhere, bit-identical state across native, wasm32, and the browser (F6). Two decisions are recorded as deliberate human overrides of the analysis: E1 (sequential seeded movement over simultaneous-with-lottery) and F1 (minimal visibility over full transparency — "poker, not chess"). The one explicitly novel, untested mechanic is E7's chosen-allegiance tribes; it ships feature-flagged.

1 · Principles

  • P1 — agent-level control: every agent is driven by a submitted policy, invoked per agent; no protocol call whose subject is "all your agents."
  • P2 — the simulation defines physics; policies define behavior. Config never encodes how agents act.
  • P3 — fixed pool, fixed length, scored at the end. No persistent world, no drop-in entry.
  • Founding decision: v2 is our own implementation, not a DTL port — player control is designed in from the start, not retrofitted.

P1 — Agent-level control, not population-level control. Every agent is controlled by a submitted player policy. A single policy may control more than one agent, but it is invoked per agent, with that agent's context, and makes a decision for that agent. A policy can control a population of agents; it cannot control them as a population. Two consequences worth stating: the observation given to a policy invocation is scoped to one agent (§7) — a policy does not receive a roster of its agents or their positions — and the scratchpad memory (§7) is the only protocol channel through which a policy could coordinate its own agents, which made the pad's scope a design decision with teeth (F2 resolves it, with an honest-rules caveat).

P2 — The simulation defines the physics; policies define the behavior. Config controls the environment and which mechanics exist (§4). It does not control how agents behave: no movement rules, no aggression parameters, no lending rates or durations. Where classic Sugarscape hard-codes a behavioral rule, v2 exposes a decision to the policy instead. The permitted exceptions are physics-side parameters of a mechanic — e.g. combat α (§6), which bounds what combat yields, not whether an agent fights.

P3 — Fixed pool, fixed length, scored at the end. An episode starts with a fixed set of players, runs for a fixed duration, and reports a score per player at the end. The rationale is recorded because it forecloses a tempting alternative: a run-forever world accumulates wealth — through lifetime accumulation, and through inheritance if enabled — so incumbents dominate on tenure rather than policy quality, and a newly entering policy faces an unfair, uninteresting test. Age-based die-offs don't fix this. Real life has this property; a competitive benchmark shouldn't.

Decision — Founding (platform decision D1)

v2 is our own Sugarscape, not a port of the DTL Python model. DTL docs/dtl-implementation.html and the retired v1 archived/v1/ are reference material for what mechanics exist, not templates. The central motivation: player control of agents must be designed in from the start, with clear gates, rather than retrofitted — the v1 attempt demonstrated the retrofit ceiling concretely. (Distinct from lifecycle decision D1, max age, in §5.)

2 · Players, Seats, and Agents

  • Up to 64 seats; a seat is one submitted policy and may control many agents, always invoked per-agent (P1).
  • A1 — total_agents is config (default 64); the seat count must divide it; equal allocation holds at t=0 only.
  • A2 — newborn seat assignment is configurable; ranked default random_parent (episode-seeded 50/50).
  • A3 — a faulted seat's agents freeze: they act in no phase, but physics continues.

Seats and totals (A1). An episode admits up to 64 seats, each one submitted policy. Config declares total_agents (default 64); validation enforces total_agents % seats == 0, and each seat starts with total_agents / seats agents — equal allocation at t=0. Making the total itself config keeps agent density a per-variant/league dial (density drives combat and trade pressure), lets the certification fixture run tiny (e.g. 4 agents across 2 seats, against the ranked default of 64), and preserves the Arena stress range: 64 seats means 65 component instances (64 players + 1 game) at the heavy end, while a 4-seat episode is the gentle case docs/v2-design.md §2.

Newborn assignment (A2). When mating (E5) creates an agent mid-episode, P1 demands it belong to some seat. The controlling seat is chosen per the config option newborn_assignment:

ModeChild goes to
random_parentOne of the two parents' seats, episode-seeded 50/50 — ranked default (settled with B5)
initiating_parentThe seat whose agent initiated the mating
receiving_parentThe seat whose agent was initiated to (closer to human mating dynamics — offspring stays with the receiving parent)

Under the sum reduce (B5) a child is upside for whichever seat receives it, so the neutral 50/50 keeps E5's negotiation symmetric. The two directional modes make the two sides of a mating structurally different — that incentive asymmetry is now a deliberate variant-design lever, not a flaw. Note that equal allocation is strictly a t=0 property: a policy good at mating ends up invoking on more agents over time, which is both a reward and a compute asymmetry, accepted knowingly docs/v2-design.md §8.1.

Dropped seats freeze (A3). When a seat faults (Arena drops it permanently; the WS shell mirrors the same rule), its agents act in no phase from then on. Physics continues: they metabolize, age, starve, and die per D2, and other agents may exploit them — as combat targets, or simply as bodies occupying cells. There is no sim-owned fallback behavior: a frozen agent is indistinguishable from one whose policy legally chooses inaction every phase (E9 makes inaction legal precisely so this rule needs no special code path). This kills v1's sim-side greedy fallback for good — v1 answered a disconnected or timed-out player by picking the move itself archived/v1/src/sugarscape/coworld.nim:467-488, which is exactly the behavior P2 evicts. The platform separately records the fault (SeatFault, failed_policy_index); the freeze rule is only the in-world consequence. This closes fork 6 of docs/what-is-a-coworld.md.

3 · Scoring

  • Per-agent wellness = time-integrated Cobb–Douglas welfare: Σ of per-tick welfare ÷ T, where T is episode length (ranked default 1000 ticks); death truncates the integral (B2+B3).
  • Cobb–Douglas exponents = metabolism shares, the classic form (B4); holdings stay gross of debt — E6 closes that deferral.
  • Per-seat score = a configurable reduce over the seat's agents; ranked default sum (B5) — the one reduce where survival, prosperity, and reproduction all point the same way.
  • Ties break on surviving agent count, then a genuine draw (B6), encoded lexicographically below welfare precision in the score scalar.

Episode length (B1). The ranked default is 1000 timesteps. Against D1's max-age default of [60, 100] ticks that yields roughly 10–15 generations, plus 10+ season cycles when seasons are enabled, and a deep horizon for trade and lending dynamics. It is about half of v1's 2000-tick league episodes — the compute saved is spent on more episodes per round. Certification fixtures stay tiny, around 10–20 ticks docs/v2-design.md §4.

Per-tick welfare (B4). Each tick, each living agent's welfare is the classic Cobb–Douglas form over its holdings w and metabolisms m: welfare = Π wᵣ^(mᵣ/mT), with mT = Σ mᵣ — an agent that needs more spice weighs spice more. Exponents-as-metabolism-shares was uncontested: it is the established, trade-generating choice from the literature. The one deferred sub-question — gross holdings or net of loan principal — was deliberately pushed into E6, which resolved it: welfare is computed over gross holdings (§6, E6).

Time-integrated wellness (B2+B3). An agent's wellness for the episode is the sum of its per-tick welfare over the whole episode, divided by T — the episode length, not ticks-alive. Ticks after death contribute 0, and dead agents remain in the reduce with their truncated integrals. The consequences are the reason for the choice: death costs proportionally to how early it happens (no cliff, no one-death-zeroes-everything); dying rich early doesn't pay, because the integral simply stops — normalizing by ticks-alive would resurrect exactly that exploit, hence ÷T; culling weak agents doesn't pay, because they stay in the reduce; and newborns (A2) integrate naturally with a bounded contribution. Implementation is one running welfare sum per agent; the observation (§7) exposes both current-tick welfare and the running integral, so the quantity being optimized is legible to the policies optimizing it, and results/replays can carry per-tick welfare curves docs/v2-design.md §3.

Decision — B2+B3 (2026-08-07)

Wellness is integrated over the episode, ÷T; dead agents stay in the reduce. Every end-snapshot variant examined had a disqualifying pathology (see §10); integration dissolves all of them at once rather than patching any one. This resolved the design tension recorded as §8.2 in docs/v2-design.md.

The reduce (B5). The config names a reduce function; the game applies it to the wellness integrals of every agent the seat controlled at any point:

score_reduceMeaningCharacter as a game
sumTotal integrated welfare across the seat's agents, ÷T — ranked defaultSurvival, prosperity, and reproduction all point the same way; bounded by carrying capacity
meanArithmetic meanVariant lever: wealth strategy without population growth — structurally punishes mating (late-born agents dilute)
maxBest single agentVariant only: 15 of 16 agents irrelevant; invites culling-by-neglect
minWorst single agentVariant only: every newborn is your minimum
medianMiddle agentVariant only: tail-blind

Decision — B5 (2026-08-07)

Ranked default is sum — added to the table for this purpose; it was not among the original candidates. It is the only reduce under which a child is worth the welfare it goes on to accrue, death is its own penalty, and culling never pays. The total-utilitarian failure mode is capped physically: the map can only sustain so many agents. A rider settled with it: ranked newborn_assignment = random_parent (keeps E5's negotiation symmetric; the directional modes are variant levers).

Ties (B6). Equal integrals → the seat with more agents alive at episode end ranks higher; still equal → a genuine draw, which the platform's all-pairs Elo scores 0.5/0.5 natively. Because the platform ranks on the single results.scores scalar, the survivor tie-break is encoded lexicographically into the score value — survivor count in bits strictly below welfare precision, with the exact layout owned by F6's determinism spec. Results also carry survivor counts as a plain field for human reading docs/v2-design.md §3.

4 · The World and Its Configuration

  • Every row below is decided config surface; a capacity map is data, never a program (C1).
  • Every world has both sugar and spice state (C5); "sugar-only" = zero spice capacity + zero spice metabolism.
  • Seasons, pollution, and disease are the classic mechanics with all-integer semantics (C2–C4), each feature-flagged.
  • Movement rules, aggression parameters, and lending rate/duration are explicitly not config — that is behavior, and behavior belongs to policies (P2).

Two rejections shaped this table. Observation-only pollution was rejected because under P2 it is provably inert: a mechanic that only appears in observations, with no physical consequence, is costless data that a smart policy simply ignores — the classic's pollution "worked" only because the hard-coded movement rule was forced to respect it. And a procedural custom-map spec (v1-style radial-falloff peaks) was rejected because the falloff math would become normative spec that every runtime must reproduce bit-for-bit; explicit grids keep the determinism surface flat docs/v2-design.md §4.

Explicitly not config: movement rules, aggression parameters, lending rate and duration. Action selection belongs to policies; lending terms are a negotiation between agents (E6).

5 · Agent Lifecycle

  • D1 — max age drawn per agent from config range [60, 100] ticks (ranked default); null = immortal, a variant lever. With 1000-tick episodes this forces ~10–15 generations.
  • D2/D3 — death by starvation, old age, or combat; no replacement — reproduction (E5) is the only source of new agents.
  • D4 — estates split by integer division among living children, remainder (or the whole estate) drops on the death cell; feature-flagged, ranked default on.
  • D5 — full classic genetics: crossover for vision, metabolisms, and immune strings; each parent pays half of its own initial endowment as the child's starting wealth.

An agent has position, sugar and spice holdings, per-resource metabolism, vision, age, and whatever the enabled mechanics add: tribe, immune state, fertility attributes, loans. At t=0 each agent draws, seeded, from configured ranges: initial holdings, metabolisms, vision, max age, and — when fertility is on — sex, fertility window (childbearing start/end ages), and an immune bit-string (C4) docs/v2-design.md §5.

Max age (D1). Drawn per-agent at episode start from the config range max_age: [min, max], classic ranked default [60, 100] ticks; null means immortal and is a variant lever, not ranked play. This is the partner mechanism to B5's sum reduce: with 1000-tick episodes and 60–100-tick lifespans, reproduction becomes strategically necessary, not optional. Under integrated wellness, age death is amortized — a full life's welfare has already accrued — so it is never a scoring cliff.

Death and replacement (D2, D3). Death causes: starvation (cannot pay a tick's metabolism from holdings), old age, and combat when E2 resolves lethally. When death is checked inside the timestep is E8's ordering (§6). There is no replacement: P3's fixed pool plus P1 (every agent belongs to a seat) preclude classic batch-style replacement; reproduction is the only source of new agents.

Estates (D4). A dead agent's holdings split by integer division among its living children — possibly cross-seat, per A2 — and the remainder, or the whole estate when no child lives, drops on the death cell as harvestable resource. Cell levels may exceed capacity; growback simply doesn't apply above capacity. Inheritance is feature-flagged, ranked default on. Under sum, inheritance makes mating an investment rather than just a lottery. Epstein & Axtell's caution that inheritance amplifies inequality doesn't bite inside one bounded episode (P3). Lending hooks in here: E6 ties outstanding debts to heirs (§6).

Newborn genetics (D5). Newborns follow the full classic rule S: vision and each metabolism by per-attribute 50/50 crossover; the immune string by bitwise crossover (inherited resistance); and each parent contributes half of its own initial endowment as the child's starting wealth — mating's price tag, and the reason E5 gates fertility on wealth. Max age, sex, and fertility window are drawn fresh. The consequence: genes and estates both flow through lineages, so mate choice is genetic strategy, episode-bounded per P3 — and partially benefits rival seats via A2's cross-seat children.

6 · The Timestep and Its Action Gates

  • A phased timestep across the whole population — not the classic one-agent-does-everything sequential turn: world physics → five action gates → upkeep (E8).
  • Each gate is a named point where policies are consulted, with a defined observation before it and a defined resolution after it — the structure DTL lacked and v1 could not retrofit.
  • Movement/combat run sequentially in episode-seeded order (E1 — a recorded human override); trade, mating, and lending share one propose/respond negotiation shape (E4/E5/E6).
  • Inaction is legal at every gate, and illegal actions resolve as no-ops (E9) — A3's freeze rule depends on this.

Classic Sugarscape (and DTL) activates one agent at a time, which does everything — move, harvest, mate, trade — before the next agent acts. v2 runs phases across the whole population instead. What this buys: each phase is a clean gate — a named point where policies are consulted, with a defined observation before it and a defined resolution after it. That is precisely the structure DTL lacked docs/dtl-implementation.html and v1 could not retrofit — v1 exposed one gate, movement, because that is all its closed 14-step agent turn allowed archived/v1/.

Load-bearing ordering choices (E8). Metabolism at end of tick: an agent can harvest in the morning to pay for dinner; starvation is failing to feed yourself despite acting — the classic collect-then-metabolize grace, phased. Loans before metabolism: today's debts are collectible from today's earnings, but senior to dinner — you can starve by repaying, so over-leverage is genuinely dangerous, while E6's write-off keeps lender risk real too. Welfare accrues once per tick, post-upkeep, after deaths: the day's actions count the same day, and a death tick contributes 0, consistent with B2.

Inaction is legal at every gate (E9). No-move / no-action is always a valid choice — the classic's forced best-cell move was behavior, and it is evicted. Illegal actions (an out-of-range move, an ineligible attack, an unpayable offer) resolve as no-ops, identical to choosing inaction, and are never penalized beyond their own opportunity cost. A3's freeze rule depends on this: a faulted seat's agents are simply agents that choose inaction forever.

Within-phase order (E1)

Different phases use different activation disciplines. Movement — and therefore combat — is sequential, in an episode-seeded order reshuffled each timestep. Each agent observes fully-updated state at its turn; an occupied cell is simply occupied — no conflict rules, classic semantics. Harvest is order-free (own-cell only, no agent interaction) and is delivered as one concurrent fan-out. Trade and mating are negotiation phases whose within-phase structure belongs to their protocol designs (E4/E5), explicitly not a simple activation-order question. Tribe declaration is order-free. This closes fork 5's residue in docs/what-is-a-coworld.md.

Decision — E1 (2026-08-07) · Human override

Sequential seeded movement, by James's explicit call — overriding the recommendation. The assistant recommended simultaneous movement with a conflict lottery, on fairness and latency grounds. James chose sequential seeded order: classic Sugarscape semantics were valued above the WS-shell latency cost, and the cost is consciously accepted — N serial policy round-trips per timestep is negligible under Arena (in-process) but slow for the WS shell at full scale. Arena is the primary runtime; the WS shell serves certification and local play at small configs.

Movement and combat (E2)

The movement action names a target cell; if the cell is occupied and combat is enabled, the same action is an attack — there is no separate combat verb. Legality is physics: the target must be strictly poorer (wealth = sugar + spice, an unweighted integer sum) and, when tribes are enabled, of a different tribe. An eligible attacker deterministically wins: the victim dies (D2), the attacker loots min(α, victim wealth) and takes the cell, and the victim's remaining estate flows through D4 — combat and inheritance compose with no extra rule. Wealth doubles as armor: the poorer-only rule prevents kamikaze deletion of the rich. The classic retaliation look-ahead is evicted as behavior, not dropped as an oversight: the sim permits any legal attack, and risk assessment belongs to policies — which is why F1 must expose neighbor wealth. Illegal attack attempts resolve like illegal moves, per E9.

Harvest (E3)

Unlike every classic Sugarscape variant, harvesting is explicit and optional: an agent on a resource cell chooses whether to harvest, and the action names per-resource amounts, clamped to the cell's current level. All-or-nothing stays expressible — request everything. What partial amounts buy is priceable restraint: harvesting exactly metabolic need minimizes C3's pollution production, self-toxicity, and growback suppression. Sustainable-yield farming is the strategy this enables; not harvesting at all is also meaningful (leaving sugar to grow, or denying pollution production under a pollution regime).

Trade (E4)

The trade phase runs trade_rounds propose/respond cycles (default 2–3, a placeholder pending playtesting). In each cycle, every agent may submit one offer targeted at one von-Neumann-adjacent agent — {give: (sugar, spice), want: (sugar, spice)}, integer bundles, give clamped to holdings. Each agent then accepts or declines the offers addressed to it; acceptances execute in episode-seeded order, voiding any offer no longer payable. One-offer-per-proposer kills double-spend on the proposing side; voiding handles acceptor overcommitment. Countering is structural, not special-cased: decline, and propose back next cycle. A cycle with zero offers ends the phase early — quiescence is a trivial deterministic check, not a definition problem.

Decision — E4 (2026-08-07)

Bilateral targeted offers over R cycles; the sim never computes MRS or prices. Prices emerge, or they don't. Spot barter only — credit is E6's mechanic. Cross-tick price discovery is expected, and the scratchpad (§7) is where reputation lives. Order-book matching and free-form negotiation channels remain buildable later as variants on the same encoding.

Mating (E5)

Mating reuses the E4 consent shape: mating_rounds propose/respond cycles, one targeted proposal per agent per cycle to an eligible adjacent partner; accept or decline; accepted matings execute in seeded order, re-validating eligibility at execution (E4's voiding rule reused); a zero-proposal cycle ends the phase early. Eligibility is physics, all classic: fertile age window; wealth ≥ own initial endowment (which makes D5's half-endowment contribution payable and limits reproduction to the successful); opposite sex (D5 draws sex when fertility is on); von Neumann adjacency; and an empty cell adjacent to either parent for the child — seeded pick; if none exists the mating voids, the classic crowding cap. One delta from classic: each agent completes at most one mating per timestep, which closes tick-scale Genghis strategies — lineage volume lives across ticks. Mate choice is the strategy layer D5 built: proposing to good genes is investment, and accepting prices in the endowment cost and A2's coin-flip seat assignment.

Lending (E6)

Lending is the third instance of the E4 shape: a proposal is a credit contract {lend: (s, sp), repay: (s, sp), due_tick} offered to an adjacent agent; acceptance transfers the principal now. Interest is implicit and per-contract — repay exceeds lend by whatever the parties negotiate. The physics: due_tick ≤ episode end (no contracting past P3's horizon); at due, the sim auto-collects min(owed, holdings); a shortfall closes the contract — the lender eats the loss, so credit risk prices into negotiated interest. On death, heirs inherit debt pro-rata with the estate, capped at what they inherited (limited liability); with no heirs, the debt is written off. Anyone holding goods may lend — the classic age/fertility matchmaking is evicted as behavior. E6 also closes B4's deferred sub-question: welfare stays over gross holdings. Under integrated welfare with due ≤ T and auto-collect, interest is the price of welfare-time and the market sets it; even Cobb–Douglas concavity gains — rich→poor transfers raise total welfare, including via E4 gifts — are legitimate distribution management under sum, not exploits.

Tribes (E7)

A tribe is a declared affiliation — not emergent culture (the classic tags) and not static teams (v1's seat-tribes). Config sets tribe_count (K, ranked default 2); initial assignment is episode-seeded; and phase 5 is real — an agent may declare a switch, effective at the next timestep. Combat gates on cross-tribe (E2). Tribes are protection pacts: safety-in-numbers, defection, and betrayal — leave today, attack your ex-tribemate tomorrow — become policy strategy. Feature-flagged like the other mechanics; tribeless variants gate combat on wealth alone.

Decision — E7 (2026-08-07)

Chosen allegiance is the design's one explicitly novel mechanic — and its biggest untested bet. There is no literature precedent; the dynamics will be observed in playtesting, and the feature flag is the fallback if they disappoint (§11). It was chosen over classic cultural tags (emergent and passive — nearly invisible to a policy trying to play them, and no phase 5 at all) and v1's seat-tribes (which degenerate to everyone-their-own-tribe at 64 single-agent seats — zero strategic content).

7 · The Policy Boundary

  • F1 — minimal visibility (a recorded human override): visible agents show only seat, holdings, tribe, and sickness; genes, age, sex, fertility, and max age are hidden — poker, not chess.
  • F2 — each agent carries one persistent scratchpad, 8 KiB of UTF-8 (config-adjustable), with an honest-rules clause: intra-seat RAM coordination is legal because it is unenforceable to ban.
  • F3 — every message is UTF-8 JSON in a versioned envelope, encoded by pure core functions shared by all shells.
  • A4 — the game has no notion of wall-clock time; deadlines that produce faults belong to the runtime.

Observation (F1)

Geometry: classic von Neumann rays. An agent sees the four axis rays of length vision — no diagonals — and vision is movement range: movement targets any visible cell, and an occupied target is combat per E2. Visible cells show current sugar and spice levels and pollution.

Visible agents: minimal attributes. Each visible occupant shows only {seat, holdings (sugar, spice), tribe, sick} — the minimums E2 (wealth legality), E4 (counterparty holdings), and C4 (sickness) require. Genes (metabolisms, vision), age, sex, and fertility are hidden; max_age is private to everyone, including its owner's observers. Mate and rival assessment is inference, not reading: voided proposals carry information, and the scratchpad is where courtship and reputation knowledge accumulates. The fog is both spatial and attribute-deep.

Own state: complete. Holdings, metabolisms, vision, age, own max age, sex, fertility window, tribe, sickness and immune state, outstanding loan contracts in both directions, position — plus current-tick welfare and the running integral, so the score being optimized is legible (B2).

Globals: current tick, episode length T, and season state when enabled. The public game config — including the capacity map — is handed to every policy at episode start: it is public anyway, since it ships in replays. The map is known; the other agents are the uncertainty.

Decision — F1 (2026-08-07) · Human override

Minimal visibility, by James's explicit call — overriding the recommendation. The assistant recommended full transparency of agent attributes; James chose the minimal set — "poker, not chess." The consequence is flagged and accepted: mating proposals are partially blind (a proposer cannot read a target's fertility), voided proposals are information, and the scratchpad is where courtship inference accumulates. If playtesting shows courtship is too random, exposing a fertile_now bit is a one-line revision (§11). Square-radius vision was also rejected — it would break the vision = movement-range identity.

Memory: the scratchpad (F2)

Each agent has one persistent scratchpad: 8 KiB (config-adjustable) of validated UTF-8 text, delivered with that agent's observation and rewritable with its action. Because Arena components are sandboxed WASM with no filesystem, the pad lives in the host–policy contract itself — observation carries the blob in, the action result carries it back — which makes its size cap a protocol bandwidth knob. Pads don't touch world state, so they are not needed for replay determinism; whether replays optionally snapshot them was F5's call (excluded from the base format, §8).

Honest-rules clause The Arena research corrected an earlier premise: component instances (and WS player processes) live for the whole episode, so seat-scoped RAM persists regardless — per-agent pad scope was never an enforceable isolation boundary, and v2 doesn't pretend it is. Intra-seat coordination through private memory is legal (unenforceable to ban); P1 governs the invocation shape (per-agent observations and actions), not memory isolation. The pad's real value is legibility: protocol-owned, per-agent-keyed memory — inspectable in debugging, replay-snapshottable, and the natural home for courtship/reputation inference under F1's minimal visibility. Stateless-per-invocation policy styles (fresh-context LLM calls) read their pad instead of re-deriving docs/v2-design.md §7.2, §8.3.

Encoding (F3)

All observation, action, and negotiation messages are UTF-8 JSON with a versioned envelope ({v, type, ...}), schema documented in PROTOCOL.md, encoded and decoded by pure core functions shared by every shell. The rationale: zero-codegen authoring in any language, LLM-direct legibility, and eyeball debuggability. Under F1's minimal observations, messages are small — roughly 1–4 KB — and the policy boundary is not the hot loop. A binary protocol version can be added behind the same seam if profiling ever demands it. This matches every turn-based coworld in the ecosystem, including v1, and closes fork 1 of docs/what-is-a-coworld.md.

Time (A4)

The game has no notion of wall-clock time and imposes no per-invocation budget. A policy is either responsive or faulted (→ A3 freeze); deadlines that produce faults belong to the runtime — Arena's host per-delivery deadline, or the WS shell's per-decision timeout (config, on the order of seconds). This is forced as well as chosen: a deterministic Arena guest has no clock, so game-enforced time limits are inexpressible there, and any game-level budget would make the two runtimes play different rules. One deployment (not model) consequence: Arena runners for Sugarscape should set the per-invocation deadline to roughly 1–5 s — the 180 s host default is sized for CTF/LLM profiles — since the episode wall clock (3600 s under Arena) is the only other bound on a slow-but-legal policy. Per-policy compute fairness is the platform's job (fixed CPU per component), not the game's docs/v2-design.md §7.5.

8 · Runtime and Replay Architecture

  • F4 — one deterministic core with thin shells, built in order: core → Arena components → WS container → replay viewer.
  • Arena is the dogfood and the smallest harness for the core; the WS shell remains mandatory for certification and league hosting (Arena is experiment-scoped).
  • F5 — a replay is config + seed + decision log; the viewer is a static bundle that resimulates with the same core compiled for the browser.
  • All six platform design forks from docs/what-is-a-coworld.md are closed by this design.

Runtime (F4). One deterministic core, thin shells, built in this order: core → Arena components (softmax:game / softmax:player, per the merged coworld-ctf recipe coworld-ctf/arena/) → WS container shell → replay viewer. Arena metta docs/specs/0076-arena-single-pod-experiment.md is the dogfood and the smallest harness that proves the core; the WS shell remains mandatory for certification and league hosting, because Arena is experiment-scoped, and it serves small configs while accepting E1's sequential-movement latency. The manifest omits engine_runtime — v2 builds on none of the listed runtimes. Closes fork 2.

Replay (F5). A replay is config + seed + decision log — every accepted action and offer per gate, in execution order — in a versioned JSON envelope consistent with F3, compressed. It is emitted incrementally via Arena's replay-append and written to COGAME_SAVE_REPLAY_URI by the WS shell: same bytes either way. The viewer is a static bundle that resimulates with the same core compiled for the browser — v1's own signpost, and the ctf/crewrift production pattern. Scratchpad snapshots are excluded from the base format: they are not needed for resimulation, and policy memory is not broadcast by default; a flagged debug/spectator extension may add them later. The Emscripten-vs-jco viewer build path is an implementation choice, not spec. Closes fork 3.

9 · Determinism Spec

  • Same config + seed + decision log ⇒ bit-identical world state on every target: native, Arena wasm32, and the browser viewer (F6).
  • No floats anywhere in the game — welfare is computed in 32.32 fixed point via pinned ln/exp implementations.
  • RNG: one master u64 seed hashed into per-subsystem PCG-class streams with explicit uint64 state.
  • A canonical per-tick state hash, recorded into replays, makes native↔wasm↔viewer parity a testable gate.

The spec has four planks docs/v2-design.md §7.7:

  1. All-integer world state. No floats anywhere in the game, including welfare: Cobb–Douglas is computed in fixed-point (32.32) via pinned polynomial/table ln/exp implementations — welfare = exp((m₁·ln w₁ + m₂·ln w₂)/mT). No libm, and no FMA/contraction discipline: v1's -ffp-contract=off era ends. Scores are exact integers, with B6's survivor bits assembled arithmetically, and results JSON carries exact integers.
  2. RNG. A master u64 seed derives per-subsystem streams — movement shuffle, lotteries, endowments, disease, child placement — by hashing (master, subsystem-id); PCG-class generators with explicit uint64 state, never platform-width int (the wasm32 32-bit trap). Per-subsystem streams keep behavior stable under feature toggles: switching disease off must not perturb the movement shuffle.
  3. Defined iteration order everywhere. Arrays and explicit sort keys; no hash-table ordering may leak into behavior.
  4. Canonical per-tick state hash, recorded periodically into the replay; native↔wasm↔viewer hash parity tests are the correctness gate — the coworld-ctf gameHash pattern coworld-ctf/arena/.
Decision — F6 (2026-08-07)

All-integer, with fixed-point welfare — not floats with discipline. Vendored-pow floating point was rejected because floats would re-enter the accumulating welfare integral and the contraction-flag discipline would return with them; deferring to a minimal spec was rejected because replay exactness remains unproven until this exact choice is made anyway. Completes fork 4 of docs/what-is-a-coworld.md.

10 · Alternatives Considered

  • Every decision below records the losing branches and the specific reason each lost — the design record in docs/v2-design.md often keeps only the winner.
  • Two entries (E1, F1) are human overrides of the analysis and are marked as such.
  • Recurring theme: any alternative that put behavior back inside the simulation lost to P2.

A — Players and seats

A1 — total agent count. Rejected a fixed total of 64 (certification would have to simulate 64 agents; no density variants) and an agents_per_seat-only parametrization — James preferred the explicit total plus divisor validation.

A2 — newborn assignment. Rejected any single fixed rule in favor of the configurable three-mode set. Also rejected: sim-controlled children (reintroduces v1's two-tier NPC wart and violates P1/P2) and fertility-off-in-ranked (punts the question rather than answering it).

A3 — dropped seats. Rejected immediate death (infra noise would shock the ecology discontinuously for everyone else) and sim-owned fallback behavior — v1's greedy fallback generalized archived/v1/src/sugarscape/coworld.nim:467-488 — which would require the sim to implement exactly the behavior P2 evicted. Freeze wins because physics continues and no special code path exists.

A4 — budgets. Rejected game-enforced time budgets entirely: inexpressible under Arena (a deterministic guest has no clock), and any game-level budget would make the two runtimes play different rules. Time is runtime-owned.

B — Episode and scoring

B1 — length. Rejected 500 ticks (mating and lending dynamics too shallow) and 2000 ticks (v1's league value — doubles compute and squeezes the 3600 s Arena episode wall clock).

B2+B3 — wellness. Rejected all end-snapshot variants: dead-scores-0 (one death zeroes min; a loss at t=999 erases the whole episode), welfare-at-death (dying rich early scores like living rich long), and exclude-the-dead (culling your weak agents raises the reduce — monstrous). Also rejected integrating but normalizing by ticks-alive: a short spectacular life would outrank a long good one. Integration ÷ T dissolves every one of these pathologies at once.

B4 — Cobb–Douglas form. Exponents were uncontested (metabolism shares — the classic, trade-generating choice). The gross-vs-net question was deliberately deferred into E6 rather than decided early — and E6 resolved it: gross.

B5 — reduce. Rejected mean as ranked default (structurally punishes mating — late-born agents dilute the average), max (15 of a seat's 16 agents become irrelevant; invites culling-by-neglect), min (every newborn is your minimum), and median/geometric_mean (tail-blind; one near-zero agent collapses the geomean). sum was added to the table as the only reduce where survival, prosperity, and reproduction all point the same way, bounded by carrying capacity.

B6 — ties. James rejected both pure draws and full deterministic tie-break chains; chose survivors-then-draw. Implementation note: the survivor count encodes lexicographically below welfare precision in the score scalar — F6 owns the exact layout.

C — World mechanics

C1 — maps. Rejected a v1-style procedural-peaks spec (radial falloff math becomes normative spec every runtime must reproduce bit-for-bit) and supporting both formats (two validation and determinism stories). A map is data, not a program.

C2 — seasons. Rejected fractional winter growback rates (floats in the environment) and per-resource climates (no classic precedent; doubles the config surface).

C3 — pollution. Rejected observation-only pollution — provably inert under P2, since smart policies ignore costless data; the classic's pollution only worked through the hard-coded movement rule. Shipped both physical effects, independently configurable, rather than picking one story.

C4 — disease. Rejected a simpler SIR model (flatter dynamics, generic rather than specific immunity) and deferral (leaves holes in the config schema and in F1's observation contract).

C5 — resources. James rejected a first-class single-resource mode; chose always-both. Uniform schemas everywhere; sugar-only is just zero spice capacity plus zero spice metabolism, and no trade validation is needed since trade is policy-negotiated — a spiceless world has nothing to exchange.

D — Agent lifecycle

D1 — max age. Rejected ranked-immortal and no-max-age: well-played agents would live all 1000 ticks, fertility would become optional, and B5's reproduction incentive would be undermined.

D2–D4 — death and estates. Rejected inherit-else-vanish and always-vanish (deflationary; corpses inert) and always-drop (removes the dynasty investment mechanic). No-replacement was confirmed directly from P3 + P1.

D5 — genetics. Rejected all-fresh draws for newborns (mate choice becomes strategically empty — hollows E5) and a configurable genetics knob (an untested second path with no demand).

E — Actions and phases

E1 — activation. The assistant recommended simultaneous movement with a conflict lottery (fairness, latency); James overrode the recommendation and chose sequential seeded order for movement — classic semantics valued over the WS-shell latency cost, consciously accepted (Arena is the primary runtime). Harvest and tribe are order-free; the negotiation phases get their own structures in E4/E5.

E2 — combat. Rejected probabilistic resolution by wealth ratio (adds outcome RNG and weakens wealth-as-armor) and unrestricted attack (kamikaze griefing becomes free for doomed agents). The classic retaliation look-ahead is evicted as behavior — not dropped as an oversight.

E3 — harvest. Rejected all-or-nothing harvesting: pollution restraint would collapse to a binary choice, and C3's levers would lose their play.

E4 — trade. Rejected a sim-matched order book (the matcher re-centralizes what P2 decentralized, and bilateral strategy — partners, reputation, boycott — dissolves) and a free-form byte negotiation channel (protocol surface explodes and the binding machinery is still needed; buildable later as a variant).

E5 — mating. Rejected dropping the opposite-sex requirement (makes sex vestigial) and willingness-flags with sim-side pairing (partner choice disappears — hollows D5's genetic strategy).

E6 — lending. Rejected rollover-on-shortfall (zombie contracts, compounding bookkeeping) and net-of-principal welfare (belt-and-suspenders against an exploit that due ≤ T plus auto-collect already neutralize; would put principal tracking inside the scoring function). The insight worth preserving: under integrated welfare, interest is the price of welfare-time, and Cobb–Douglas concavity gains from rich→poor transfers — including E4 gifts — are legitimate distribution management under sum, not exploits.

E7 — tribes. Rejected classic cultural tags (emergent and passive — nearly invisible to a policy trying to play them; no phase 5), v1's seat-tribes (degenerates to everyone-their-own-tribe at 64 single-agent seats; zero strategic content), and deferral. Chosen allegiance is the one explicitly novel mechanic — flagged as the design's biggest untested bet (§11).

E8 — ordering. Rejected metabolism-before-actions (an agent dies before acting; breaks the classic collect-then-metabolize grace) and loan collection in phase 0 (tighter credit, lender-unfriendly).

E9 — inaction. Rejected penalizing illegal actions: a no-op is inaction, and A3's freeze rule depends on inaction being legal.

F — Policy interface and platform

F1 — observation. The assistant recommended full transparency; James overrode the recommendation and chose minimal visibility — {seat, holdings, tribe, sick} only, with max_age private to all — "poker, not chess." The consequence is flagged and accepted: mating proposals are partially blind, voided proposals are information, and the scratchpad is where courtship inference accumulates. Square-radius vision was also rejected — it breaks the vision = movement-range identity.

F2 — scratchpad. Rejected a per-seat pad (loses per-agent replay legibility) and dual per-seat + per-agent pads (machinery without demand). The corrected premise is on record: component RAM persists per-episode, so pad scope was never an enforceable isolation boundary — the honest-rules clause makes intra-seat RAM coordination legal.

F3 — encoding. Rejected CBOR/MessagePack (a decode tax on every debug session and every LLM policy) and a Sprite-v1-lineage binary format (built for 24 tps pixel streams — the wrong profile). The JSON family matches every turn-based coworld in the ecosystem, including v1.

F4 — runtime. Rejected a WS-first build order: it delays the dogfood and builds the bigger shell before the core is proven.

F5 — replay. Rejected scratchpad snapshots in the base replay format: bloat, and policy memory would be public by default.

F6 — determinism. Rejected vendored-pow floats (floats re-enter the accumulating integral; the contraction discipline returns) and a minimal-spec-defer (replay exactness stays unproven until the choice is made anyway).

11 · Open Questions and Risks

  • The design is complete — nothing below blocks implementation — but real risks remain, and each has a named mitigation or escape hatch.
  • The two watch-items are the two most original calls: E7's chosen-allegiance tribes and F1's minimal visibility.
  • Several defaults (negotiation round counts, combat α, pollution coefficients, tribe count) are placeholders pending playtesting.
Open — E7 chosen-allegiance tribes The one mechanic with no literature precedent; its dynamics are unobserved until playtesting. Fallback: the feature flag — tribeless variants gate combat on wealth alone, and ranked play can ship without tribes if the dynamics disappoint.
Open — F1 blind mating proposals Minimal visibility means a proposer cannot read a target's fertility. If playtesting shows courtship is too random, exposing a fertile_now bit on visible agents is a one-line revision to the observation schema.
Risk — Arena scaling 64 seats means 65 Arena component instances (64 players + 1 game), against the 17 the arena-runner experiment was sized for. A scaling question to raise with the Arena work — per-component memory caps are host constants, not game config metta docs/specs/0076-arena-single-pod-experiment.md.
Risk — Arena is experiment-scoped Arena's metta stack was unmerged as of 2026-08-06 (the jb/arena-task-* branches). The WS shell therefore remains mandatory for league hosting; F4's build order treats Arena as the dogfood, not the sole deployment path.
Open — fixed-point ln/exp implementation The 32.32 ln/exp pair must be written and parity-tested once — native vs wasm32 vs browser — then frozen with pinned coefficients. Until then, F6's bit-identical guarantee is a spec, not a demonstrated property.
Open — negotiation round defaults trade_rounds and mating_rounds defaults (2–3 cycles per phase) are placeholders pending playtesting; they trade convergence depth against per-tick policy invocations.
Open — balance unknowns Combat α (loot cap), the six pollution coefficients, and the tribe count default (K = 2) are untested balance values; playtesting owns them. None of them changes protocol shape.
