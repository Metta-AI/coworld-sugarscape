Sugarscape v2 — Design

The settled design for our own Sugarscape: player agency built into every mechanic as an explicit gate, over one all-integer deterministic core behind thin shells — the WebSocket container for v2.0, with the WASM targets (Arena components, browser replay resimulator) as future work. For James (author/reviewer) and implementing agents.

Executive Summary

Sugarscape v2 is our own Sugarscape — not a port of the DTL Python model (platform decision D1 in docs/what-is-a-coworld.md). The founding move is a clean split the classic model never made: Epstein & Axtell hard-code agent behavior — the best-cell movement rule, MRS-crossing trade, automatic mating, the combat retaliation look-ahead — directly into the simulation. v2 evicts all of it to submitted player policies and keeps only physics. Every classic behavioral rule became either a policy gate (a named point in the timestep where each agent's policy is consulted) or a negotiation protocol between agents. That is precisely the structure DTL lacked and the retired v1 could not retrofit: v1 exposed exactly one gate, movement, because its closed 14-step agent turn allowed nothing else archived/v1/.

All 35 open design questions (A1–F6) were settled on 2026-08-07, and a review round on 2026-08-10 revised several decisions in place; this document is the record, rendered from docs/v2-design.md. The most consequential calls: scoring is time-integrated Cobb–Douglas welfare, summed across a seat's agents and divided by episode length (B2+B3+B5) — the one scheme under which survival, prosperity, and reproduction all point the same way; the timestep is phased — world physics, then six action gates, then upkeep (E8); and determinism is total — no floats anywhere, bit-identical state on every target the core is built for (F6). Two decisions — E1 (sequential seeded movement) and F1 (limited visibility, "poker, not chess") — were originally recorded as deliberate human overrides of the analysis; those override records now live in the Design History appendix (§14), and E1's rationale stands on its own terms: sequential activation guarantees every agent acts with full information. The 2026-08-10 review made observation visibility fully configurable per attribute (F1), dropped the scratchpad from the protocol (F2), moved league rating to the platform's score/EWMA standing (§3), and parked a novel "speaking" mechanic as future work (§12). Two revisions followed on 2026-08-11: F2 was re-revised to one policy instance per seat — the per-agent isolation experiment is reverted, and a seat's agents sharing instance RAM is an accepted cost — and the WASM implementation (Arena components + the browser-compiled viewer core) is deferred to future work; v2.0 ships the native core behind the WS shell (F4, §8, §12). The one explicitly novel, untested mechanic in v2.0 is E7's chosen-allegiance tribes; it ships feature-flagged.

1 · Principles

  • P1 — agent-level control: every agent is driven by a submitted policy, invoked per agent; no protocol call whose subject is "all your agents."
  • P2 — the simulation defines physics; policies define behavior. Config never encodes how agents act.
  • P3 — fixed pool, fixed length, scored at the end. No persistent world, no drop-in entry.
  • Founding decision: v2 is our own implementation, not a DTL port — player control is designed in from the start, not retrofitted.

P1 — Agent-level control, not population-level control. Every agent is controlled by a submitted player policy. A single policy may control more than one agent, but it is invoked per agent, with that agent's context, and makes a decision for that agent. A policy can control a population of agents; it cannot control them as a population. Two consequences worth stating: the observation given to a policy invocation is scoped to one agent (§7) — a policy does not receive a roster of its agents or their positions — and P1 is a protocol-shape rule, not enforced memory isolation (re-revised 2026-08-11; the 2026-08-10 per-agent-instance enforcement is reverted): one policy instance serves all of a seat's agents (§7), so those agents share the instance's RAM and can privately coordinate — an accepted cost, not a blessed channel. Cross-seat coordination still happens only through the observable world — positions, trades, gifts (and speech, when §12's future speaking phase lands).

P2 — The simulation defines the physics; policies define the behavior. Config controls the environment and which mechanics exist (§4). It does not control how agents behave: no movement rules, no aggression parameters, no lending rates or durations. Where classic Sugarscape hard-codes a behavioral rule, v2 exposes a decision to the policy instead. The permitted exceptions are physics-side parameters of a mechanic — e.g. combat α (§6), which bounds what combat yields, not whether an agent fights.

P3 — Fixed pool, fixed length, scored at the end. An episode starts with a fixed set of players, runs for a fixed duration, and reports a score per player at the end. The rationale is recorded because it forecloses a tempting alternative: a run-forever world accumulates wealth — through lifetime accumulation, and through inheritance if enabled — so incumbents dominate on tenure rather than policy quality, and a newly entering policy faces an unfair, uninteresting test. Age-based die-offs don't fix this. Real life has this property; a competitive benchmark shouldn't.

v2 is our own Sugarscape, not a port of the DTL Python model. DTL docs/dtl-implementation.html and the retired v1 archived/v1/ are reference material for what mechanics exist, not templates. The central motivation: player control of agents must be designed in from the start, with clear gates, rather than retrofitted — the v1 attempt demonstrated the retrofit ceiling concretely. (Distinct from lifecycle decision D1, max age, in §5.)

2 · Players, Seats, and Agents

  • Seats are unbounded config, up to total_agents (revised 2026-08-10, was "up to 64"); a seat is one submitted policy and may control many agents, always invoked per-agent (P1).
  • A1 — total_agents is config (default 64, no hard upper bound — classic 250-agent worlds are legitimate); the seat count must divide it; equal allocation holds at t=0 only.
  • A2 — newborn seat assignment is configurable; ranked default random_parent: a couple's first child episode-seeded 50/50, subsequent children by Thue–Morse (revised 2026-08-10).
  • A3 — a faulted seat's agents freeze: they act in no phase, but physics continues.

Seats and totals (A1). An episode admits up to total_agents seats, each one submitted policy — neither seats nor total_agents carries a hard upper bound (revised 2026-08-10, was "up to 64"), so classic-scale worlds of 250 agents are legitimate configs. The flagship ranked variant stays at 64 total pending playtesting. Config declares total_agents (default 64); validation enforces total_agents % seats == 0, and each seat starts with total_agents / seats agents — equal allocation at t=0. Making the total itself config keeps agent density a per-variant/league dial (density drives combat and trade pressure) and lets the certification fixture run tiny (e.g. 4 agents across 2 seats, against the ranked default of 64). Instance count tracks seats, not agents (F2 re-revision, 2026-08-11 — one policy instance per seat), so large total_agents configs are cheap on any runtime docs/v2-design.md §2.

Newborn assignment (A2). When mating (E5) creates an agent mid-episode, P1 demands it belong to some seat. The controlling seat is chosen per the config option newborn_assignment:

Under the sum reduce (B5) a child is upside for whichever seat receives it, so the neutral 50/50 keeps E5's negotiation symmetric. The two directional modes make the two sides of a mating structurally different — that incentive asymmetry is now a deliberate variant-design lever, not a flaw. Note that equal allocation is strictly a t=0 property: a policy good at mating ends up invoking on more agents over time, which is both a reward and a compute asymmetry, accepted knowingly docs/v2-design.md §8.1.

Dropped seats freeze (A3). When a seat faults (Arena drops it permanently; the WS shell mirrors the same rule), its agents act in no phase from then on. Physics continues: they metabolize, age, starve, and die per D2, and other agents may exploit them — as combat targets, or simply as bodies occupying cells. There is no sim-owned fallback behavior: a frozen agent is indistinguishable from one whose policy legally chooses inaction every phase (E9 makes inaction legal precisely so this rule needs no special code path). This kills v1's sim-side greedy fallback for good — v1 answered a disconnected or timed-out player by picking the move itself archived/v1/src/sugarscape/coworld.nim:467-488, which is exactly the behavior P2 evicts. The platform separately records the fault (SeatFault, failed_policy_index); the freeze rule is only the in-world consequence. This closes fork 6 of docs/what-is-a-coworld.md.

3 · Scoring

  • Per-agent wellness = time-integrated Cobb–Douglas welfare: Σ of per-tick welfare ÷ T, where T is episode length (ranked default 1000 ticks); death truncates the integral (B2+B3).
  • Cobb–Douglas exponents = metabolism shares, the classic form (B4); holdings stay gross of debt — E6 closes that deferral.
  • Per-seat score = a configurable reduce over the seat's agents; ranked default sum (B5) — the one reduce where survival, prosperity, and reproduction all point the same way.
  • Ties break on surviving agent count, then a genuine draw (B6), encoded lexicographically below welfare precision in the score scalar.
  • League rating: the platform's score ranking algorithm with an EWMA standing over episode scores — no platform modification needed (revised 2026-08-10).

Episode length (B1). The ranked default is 1000 timesteps. Against D1's max-age default of [60, 100] ticks that yields roughly 10–15 generations, plus 10+ season cycles when seasons are enabled, and a deep horizon for trade and lending dynamics. It is about half of v1's 2000-tick league episodes — the compute saved is spent on more episodes per round. Certification fixtures stay tiny, around 10–20 ticks docs/v2-design.md §4.

Per-tick welfare (B4). Each tick, each living agent's welfare is the classic Cobb–Douglas form over its holdings w and metabolisms m: welfare = Π wᵣ^(mᵣ/mT), with mT = Σ mᵣ — an agent that needs more spice weighs spice more. Exponents-as-metabolism-shares was uncontested: it is the established, trade-generating choice from the literature. The one deferred sub-question — gross holdings or net of loan principal — was deliberately pushed into E6, which resolved it: welfare is computed over gross holdings (§6, E6).

Time-integrated wellness (B2+B3). An agent's wellness for the episode is the sum of its per-tick welfare over the whole episode, divided by T — the episode length, not ticks-alive. Ticks after death contribute 0, and dead agents remain in the reduce with their truncated integrals. The consequences are the reason for the choice: death costs proportionally to how early it happens (no cliff, no one-death-zeroes-everything); dying rich early doesn't pay, because the integral simply stops — normalizing by ticks-alive would resurrect exactly that exploit, hence ÷T; culling weak agents doesn't pay, because they stay in the reduce; and newborns (A2) integrate naturally with a bounded contribution. Implementation is one running welfare sum per agent; the observation (§7) exposes both current-tick welfare and the running integral, so the quantity being optimized is legible to the policies optimizing it, and results/replays can carry per-tick welfare curves docs/v2-design.md §3.

Decision — B2+B3 (2026-08-07)

Wellness is integrated over the episode, ÷T; dead agents stay in the reduce. Every end-snapshot variant examined had a disqualifying pathology (see §10); integration dissolves all of them at once rather than patching any one. This resolved the design tension recorded as §8.2 in docs/v2-design.md.

The reduce (B5). The config names a reduce function; the game applies it to the wellness integrals of every agent the seat controlled at any point:

Ranked default is sum — added to the table for this purpose; it was not among the original candidates. It is the only reduce under which a child is worth the welfare it goes on to accrue, death is its own penalty, and culling never pays. The total-utilitarian failure mode is capped physically: the map can only sustain so many agents. A rider settled with it: ranked newborn_assignment = random_parent (keeps E5's negotiation symmetric; the directional modes are variant levers).

League rating (revised 2026-08-10). Ranked leagues use the platform's score-based ranking, not Elo: Elo's all-pairs win/loss comparison is a poor fit for a game whose output is a continuous welfare scalar, and the platform already supports exactly what we want with no modification — league ladder config ranking.algorithm = "score" with standing_aggregation = "ewma" and a half_life_hours metta app_backend v2/ladders/rankings/score.py metta app_backend v2/ladders/config.py. The leaderboard standing is an EWMA over final episode scores; within one league all episodes run the same variant, so raw scores are comparable across episodes. Division promotion and relegation under the score algorithm use standing thresholds, already enforced by platform validation.

Ties (B6). Equal integrals → the seat with more agents alive at episode end ranks higher; still equal → a genuine draw. (Less central under score/EWMA ranking than it would have been under Elo, but the platform still compares scores, so the encoding stays.) Because the platform ranks on the single results.scores scalar, the survivor tie-break is encoded lexicographically into the score value — survivor count in bits strictly below welfare precision, with the exact layout owned by F6's determinism spec. Results also carry survivor counts as a plain field for human reading docs/v2-design.md §3.

5 · Agent Lifecycle

  • D1 — max age drawn per agent from config range [60, 100] ticks (ranked default); null = immortal, a variant lever. With 1000-tick episodes this forces ~10–15 generations.
  • D2/D3 — death by starvation, old age, or combat; no replacement — reproduction (E5) is the only source of new agents.
  • D4 — estates split by integer division among living children; two independent flags, inheritance and drop_uninherited, ranked default both on (revised 2026-08-10).
  • D5 — full classic genetics: crossover for vision, metabolisms, and immune strings; each parent pays half of its own initial endowment as the child's starting wealth.

An agent has position, sugar and spice holdings, per-resource metabolism, vision, age, and whatever the enabled mechanics add: tribe, immune state, fertility attributes, loans. At t=0 each agent draws, seeded, from configured ranges: initial holdings, metabolisms, vision, max age, and — when fertility is on — sex, fertility window (childbearing start/end ages), and an immune bit-string (C4) docs/v2-design.md §5.

Max age (D1). Drawn per-agent at episode start from the config range max_age: [min, max], classic ranked default [60, 100] ticks; null means immortal and is a variant lever, not ranked play. This is the partner mechanism to B5's sum reduce: with 1000-tick episodes and 60–100-tick lifespans, reproduction becomes strategically necessary, not optional. Under integrated wellness, age death is amortized — a full life's welfare has already accrued — so it is never a scoring cliff.

Death and replacement (D2, D3). Death causes: starvation (cannot pay a tick's metabolism from holdings), old age, and combat when E2 resolves lethally. When death is checked inside the timestep is E8's ordering (§6). There is no replacement: P3's fixed pool plus P1 (every agent belongs to a seat) preclude classic batch-style replacement; reproduction is the only source of new agents.

Estates (D4). A dead agent's holdings split by integer division among its living children — possibly cross-seat, per A2. Two independent flags govern the mechanic (revised 2026-08-10): inheritance — estates split among living children — and drop_uninherited — whatever isn't inherited (remainders, heirless estates, or everything when inheritance is off) drops on the death cell as harvestable resource; with it off, that wealth simply vanishes. Ranked default: both on. All four combinations are meaningful variants — e.g. inheritance on + drop off is dynasties without scavenging. Cell levels may exceed capacity; growback simply doesn't apply above capacity. Under sum, inheritance makes mating an investment rather than just a lottery. Epstein & Axtell's caution that inheritance amplifies inequality doesn't bite inside one bounded episode (P3). Lending hooks in here: E6 ties outstanding debts to heirs (§6).

Newborn genetics (D5). Newborns follow the full classic rule S: vision and each metabolism by per-attribute 50/50 crossover; the immune string by bitwise crossover (inherited resistance); and each parent contributes half of its own initial endowment as the child's starting wealth — mating's price tag, and the reason E5 gates fertility on wealth. Max age, sex, and fertility window are drawn fresh. The consequence: genes and estates both flow through lineages, so mate choice is genetic strategy, episode-bounded per P3 — and partially benefits rival seats via A2's cross-seat children.

6 · The Timestep and Its Action Gates

  • A phased timestep across the whole population — not the classic one-agent-does-everything sequential turn: world physics → six action gates → upkeep (E8; the lend gate was added 2026-08-11, correcting an omission in the original phase list).
  • Each gate is a named point where policies are consulted, with a defined observation before it and a defined resolution after it — the structure DTL lacked and v1 could not retrofit.
  • Movement/combat run sequentially in episode-seeded order (E1) — sequential activation is integral: every agent acts with full information; trade, mating, and lending share one propose/respond negotiation shape (E4/E5/E6), run to quiescence under a cap (default 8, revised 2026-08-10).
  • Inaction is legal at every gate, and illegal actions resolve as no-ops (E9) — A3's freeze rule depends on this.

The lend gate (added 2026-08-11, correcting an E8 omission). The original phase list was sketched on day one from the classic agent-turn verbs plus tribe, before E6 existed as a settled design; E8 then ordered that list as given, and E6 specified lending's contract semantics — with collection slotted into upkeep — without ever assigning origination a phase, even though the actions-per-gate table (§7) listed a lend gate from the start. Phase 5 is that gate. Its placement after mate is deliberate: E5's wealth-≥-endowment fertility check cannot be passed with principal borrowed the same tick — cross-tick leveraged breeding remains possible, and remains dangerous under E6's collection seniority — while placement before upkeep means distress borrowing can still pay tonight's metabolism and today's due loans.

Classic Sugarscape (and DTL) activates one agent at a time, which does everything — move, harvest, mate, trade — before the next agent acts. v2 runs phases across the whole population instead. What this buys: each phase is a clean gate — a named point where policies are consulted, with a defined observation before it and a defined resolution after it. That is precisely the structure DTL lacked docs/dtl-implementation.html and v1 could not retrofit — v1 exposed one gate, movement, because that is all its closed 14-step agent turn allowed archived/v1/.

Load-bearing ordering choices (E8). Metabolism at end of tick: an agent can harvest in the morning to pay for dinner; starvation is failing to feed yourself despite acting — the classic collect-then-metabolize grace, phased. Loans before metabolism: today's debts are collectible from today's earnings, but senior to dinner — you can starve by repaying, so over-leverage is genuinely dangerous, while E6's write-off keeps lender risk real too. Welfare accrues once per tick, post-upkeep, after deaths: the day's actions count the same day, and a death tick contributes 0, consistent with B2.

Inaction is legal at every gate (E9). No-move / no-action is always a valid choice — the classic's forced best-cell move was behavior, and it is evicted. Illegal actions (an out-of-range move, an ineligible attack, an unpayable offer) resolve as no-ops, identical to choosing inaction, and are never penalized beyond their own opportunity cost. A3's freeze rule depends on this: a faulted seat's agents are simply agents that choose inaction forever.

Within-phase order (E1)

Different phases use different activation disciplines. Movement — and therefore combat — is sequential, in an episode-seeded order reshuffled each timestep. The rationale, sharpened 2026-08-10: sequential activation is integral to the simulation, not a compromise — it guarantees every agent acts with full information. The world an agent observes is exactly the world its action applies to: no phantom moves, no conflict lotteries. The performance cost is deliberately traded for that property, and fairness is not truly sacrificed — reshuffling every tick spreads first-action benefit over the uniform distribution, and a Thue–Morse ordering over ticks is a candidate refinement for even stronger fairness (an open exploration, not committed — §12). Runtime note (revised 2026-08-11): v2.0 runs on the WS shell (the WASM/Arena implementation is deferred — §8), so sequential movement costs N policy round-trips per timestep over the socket. That latency is accepted for v2.0 — B1's 1000-tick episodes size it — and shrinks to negligible when the deferred Arena runtime's in-process calls land. Harvest is order-free (own-cell only, no agent interaction) and is delivered as one concurrent fan-out. Trade and mating are negotiation phases whose within-phase structure belongs to their protocol designs (E4/E5), explicitly not a simple activation-order question. Tribe declaration is order-free. This closes fork 5's residue in docs/what-is-a-coworld.md. (E1's original decision record, a human override of the analysis, is preserved in the Design History appendix, §14.)

Movement and combat (E2)

The movement action names a target cell; if the cell is occupied and combat is enabled, the same action is an attack — there is no separate combat verb. Legality is physics: the target must be strictly poorer (wealth = sugar + spice, an unweighted integer sum) and, when tribes are enabled, of a different tribe. An eligible attacker deterministically wins: the victim dies (D2), the attacker loots min(α, victim wealth) and takes the cell, and the victim's remaining estate flows through D4 — combat and inheritance compose with no extra rule. Wealth doubles as armor: the poorer-only rule prevents kamikaze deletion of the rich. The classic retaliation look-ahead is evicted as behavior, not dropped as an oversight: the sim permits any legal attack, and risk assessment belongs to policies — which is why F1's ranked default keeps neighbor holdings visible. Illegal attack attempts resolve like illegal moves, per E9. Hidden-holdings variant (2026-08-10): when F1's per-attribute visibility config hides holdings, the poorer-only legality check has nothing to read, so every cross-tribe attack becomes legal with resolution unchanged — the strictly-richer party wins and the loser dies, and on a tie the attacker loses, so aggression bears the risk (§7).

Harvest (E3)

Unlike every classic Sugarscape variant, harvesting is explicit and optional: an agent on a resource cell chooses whether to harvest, and the action names per-resource amounts, clamped to the cell's current level. All-or-nothing stays expressible — request everything. What partial amounts buy is priceable restraint: harvesting exactly metabolic need minimizes C3's pollution production, self-toxicity, and growback suppression. Sustainable-yield farming is the strategy this enables; not harvesting at all is also meaningful (leaving sugar to grow, or denying pollution production under a pollution regime).

Trade (E4)

The trade phase runs propose/respond cycles to quiescence with a cap (round semantics revised 2026-08-10): the phase ends when a cycle produces zero offers, or at trade_rounds cycles, whichever comes first. trade_rounds is a safety cap, not a target — the default was raised to 8 (was 2–3) because chains of offers, where the first acceptance affects the resolution of the whole chain, need enough cycles to re-propose downstream; the exact value is a playtest knob. In each cycle, every agent may submit one offer targeted at one von-Neumann-adjacent agent — {give: (sugar, spice), want: (sugar, spice)}, integer bundles, give clamped to holdings. Each agent then accepts or declines the offers addressed to it; acceptances execute in episode-seeded order, voiding any offer no longer payable. One-offer-per-proposer kills double-spend on the proposing side; voiding handles acceptor overcommitment. Countering is structural, not special-cased: decline, and propose back next cycle. A cycle with zero offers ends the phase early — quiescence is a trivial deterministic check, not a definition problem. Implementation note (it does not change semantics): agents only interact within von-Neumann-connected clusters, so each cluster's cycle can run in parallel with per-cluster seeded streams — determinism is preserved because clusters are disjoint.

Decision — E4 (2026-08-07 · round semantics rev. 2026-08-10)

Bilateral targeted offers, run to quiescence under a cap; the sim never computes MRS or prices. Prices emerge, or they don't. Spot barter only — credit is E6's mechanic. Cross-tick price discovery is expected, and the seat's persistent instance memory (§7) is where reputation lives. Order-book matching and free-form negotiation channels remain buildable later as variants on the same encoding (§12).

Mating (E5)

Mating reuses the E4 consent shape, including its revised round semantics: propose/respond cycles to quiescence under a mating_rounds cap (default 8, a playtest knob), with the same per-cluster parallelization note. One targeted proposal per agent per cycle to an eligible adjacent partner; accept or decline; accepted matings execute in seeded order, re-validating eligibility at execution (E4's voiding rule reused); a zero-proposal cycle ends the phase early. Eligibility is physics, all classic: fertile age window; wealth ≥ own initial endowment (which makes D5's half-endowment contribution payable and limits reproduction to the successful); opposite sex (D5 draws sex when fertility is on); von Neumann adjacency; and an empty cell adjacent to either parent for the child — seeded pick; if none exists the mating voids, the classic crowding cap. One delta from classic: each agent completes at most one mating per timestep, which closes tick-scale Genghis strategies — lineage volume lives across ticks. Mate choice is the strategy layer D5 built: proposing to good genes is investment, and accepting prices in the endowment cost and A2's coin-flip seat assignment.

Lending (E6)

Lending is the third instance of the E4 shape, running in its own phase-5 gate (added 2026-08-11, correcting an E8 omission — see the timestep note above) — propose/respond cycles to quiescence under a lend_rounds cap (default 8, same knob family and per-cluster parallelization as E4/E5), acceptances executed in seeded order with execution-time re-validation (both parties alive and adjacent, the lender still holding the principal). It runs in either direction (options extended 2026-08-10): a proposal is a credit contract {lend: (s, sp), repay: (s, sp), due_tick} offered to an adjacent agent, or a request — the proposer asks to borrow, the acceptor becomes the lender, and the principal flows proposer-ward on accept. Interest is implicit and per-contract — repay exceeds lend by whatever the parties negotiate. The physics: due_tick ≤ episode end (no contracting past P3's horizon). Collection at due is governed by two config knobs. The collection_floor flag chooses between collecting min(owed, holdings) (the default) and min(owed, holdings − current metabolism) — the metabolism-protected variant prevents loan upkeep from being an insta-death: repayment can't take the food out of your mouth this tick. What happens to the uncollected remainder is the shortfall_mode enum:

shortfall_modeOn shortfall
write_offThe contract closes; the lender eats the loss, so credit risk prices into negotiated interest — ranked default
renewClassic-style: the contract renews with no new transfer — principal = the uncollected remainder, repay scaled by the original contract's aggregate ratio Σrepay/Σlend (summed across resources), same duration as the original
sim_coversIf no other recourse exists (no renewal, no liable heirs), the sim makes the lender whole — mints the loss away; inflationary by design, a variant lever for credit-friendly economies

On death, heirs inherit debt pro-rata with the estate, capped at what they inherited (limited liability); with no heirs, the shortfall resolves per shortfall_mode. Anyone holding goods may lend — the classic age/fertility matchmaking is evicted as behavior. E6 also closes B4's deferred sub-question: welfare stays over gross holdings. Under integrated welfare with due ≤ T and auto-collect, interest is the price of welfare-time and the market sets it; even Cobb–Douglas concavity gains — rich→poor transfers raise total welfare, including via E4 gifts — are legitimate distribution management under sum, not exploits.

Tribes (E7)

A tribe is a declared affiliation — not emergent culture (the classic tags) and not static teams (v1's seat-tribes). Config sets tribe_count (K, ranked default 2). Initial assignment is per-seat (revised 2026-08-10): all of a seat's agents start in the same tribe, seats distributed across the K tribes by a seeded balanced permutation — seat cohesion at t=0; divergence after that is strategy. Phase 6 is real — an agent may declare a switch, effective at the next timestep. Combat gates on cross-tribe (E2). An optional variant flag adds tribeless as a declarable status (revised 2026-08-10): a tribeless agent counts as cross-tribe to everyone — attackable by all, may attack all — admitting mercenaries and loners without removing the tribe mechanic. Tribes are protection pacts: safety-in-numbers, defection, and betrayal — leave today, attack your ex-tribemate tomorrow — become policy strategy. Feature-flagged like the other mechanics; tribeless variants gate combat on wealth alone.

Decision — E7 (2026-08-07)

Chosen allegiance is the design's one explicitly novel mechanic — and its biggest untested bet. There is no literature precedent; the dynamics will be observed in playtesting, and the feature flag is the fallback if they disappoint (§11). It was chosen over classic cultural tags (emergent and passive — nearly invisible to a policy trying to play them, and no switch gate at all) and v1's seat-tribes (which degenerate to everyone-their-own-tribe at 64 single-agent seats — zero strategic content).

7 · The Policy Boundary

  • F1 — per-attribute visibility config (revised 2026-08-10): every attribute of a visible occupant is independently configurable; ranked defaults show seat, holdings, tribe, and sickness and hide the rest — poker, not chess.
  • F2 — one policy instance per seat (re-revised 2026-08-11; scratchpad stays dropped): a seat's memory is its instance RAM, shared by all its agents — the shared-RAM hole is an accepted cost, uniform across runtimes.
  • F3 — every message is UTF-8 JSON in a versioned envelope, encoded by pure core functions shared by all shells.
  • A4 — the game has no notion of wall-clock time; deadlines that produce faults belong to the runtime; interim (2026-08-10): timeouts freeze everywhere until Arena gains soft-timeout semantics.

Observation (F1)

Geometry: classic von Neumann rays. An agent sees the four axis rays of length vision — no diagonals — and vision is movement range: movement targets any visible cell, and an occupied target is combat per E2. Visible cells show current sugar and spice levels and pollution.

Visible-agent attributes: per-attribute visibility config (revised 2026-08-10, was a fixed minimal set). Every attribute of a visible occupant is independently configurable visible/hidden, so leagues can experiment with information regimes — hide holdings so price-bluffing works, or expose fertility for easier courtship. The ranked defaults reproduce the poker-not-chess regime:

Hiding an attribute changes the physics it feeds, and those rules are part of the config's meaning. Hidden holdings change combat legality (E2): the poorer-only check requires readable wealth, so when holdings are hidden every cross-tribe attack is legal and resolution is unchanged — the strictly-richer party wins and the loser dies (ties: the attacker loses; aggression bears the risk). You can attack a richer agent; you just die. The winner loots min(α, loser wealth), and the loser's remaining estate flows through D4 as usual — a deliberately sharper combat game. Hidden holdings change trade clamping (E4): offer want values are no longer clamped to counterparty holdings (you can't know them); give values remain clamped to your own, and an acceptance the acceptor cannot pay voids exactly as today — the existing execution-time voiding rule already covers it. Hidden sickness needs no special rules (C4) — it feeds no legality check; policies just lose the quarantine signal. Mate and rival assessment under hidden attributes is inference, not reading: voided proposals carry information — poker, not chess. The fog is both spatial and attribute-deep.

Own state: complete, with one knob. Holdings, metabolisms, vision, age, sex, fertility window, tribe, sickness and immune state, outstanding loan contracts in both directions, position — plus current-tick welfare and the running integral, so the score being optimized is legible (B2). Own max_age is visible to the agent itself by default, with a config flag to hide it even from self — mortality as risk rather than fact (revised 2026-08-10).

Globals: current tick, episode length T, and season state when enabled. The public game config — including the capacity map — is handed to every policy at episode start: it is public anyway, since it ships in replays. The map is known; the other agents are the uncertainty.

(F1's original decision record — minimal fixed visibility, a human override of the analysis — is preserved in the Design History appendix, §14; the 2026-08-10 revision generalized it to the per-attribute config above, whose ranked defaults reproduce the original regime.)

Memory: one policy instance per seat (F2)

One policy instance serves all of a seat's agents. The WS shell already works this way — one container per seat owns its process memory — and the deferred Arena runtime (§8, §12) will instantiate one component per seat as well. Invocation remains strictly per-agent (P1): the instance is called once per agent per gate, with that agent's observation, and answers for that agent. The shared-RAM hole is accepted, eyes open: a seat's agents share the instance's memory, so private intra-seat coordination — shared maps, role assignment, signalling outside the world — is possible. The 2026-08-10 revision closed this with per-agent component instances; that is reverted (re-revised 2026-08-11): the costs — instance count scaling with agents, spawning instances at every birth, and a rule the WS shell could never enforce anyway, making the two runtimes play different games — outweighed the enforcement. P1's memory isolation returns to the honest-rules reading, now uniform across runtimes. A seat's memory is its instance RAM, persistent across all the seat's invocations for the whole episode; a policy that wants per-agent memory keys it by agent id, and no protocol memory object is needed. Newborns need no new instance — when mating assigns a child to a seat (A2), that seat's existing instance simply starts receiving invocations for the new agent id. An in-seat child is born into whatever its policy already knows; a cross-seat child (A2's coin flip) is served by the other seat's instance and inherits nothing from its birth parents' seats except what reached the observable world (estates via D4; speech when §12's future speaking phase lands). Scaling note: instance count tracks seats (seats + 1 counting the game), independent of total_agents — 64 seats = 65 instances stays the stress case, and A1's classic-scale configs no longer multiply instances docs/v2-design.md §7.2.

Decision — F2 (re-revised 2026-08-11; supersedes the 2026-08-10 per-agent isolation)

One policy instance per seat; the scratchpad stays dropped from protocol v1. Persistent instance RAM obviates the pad's memory role entirely. What it offered beyond memory — replay-inspectable agent diaries, stateless-LLM ergonomics — was not worth a protocol object; it may return later as an opt-in debug channel if implementation misses it. A seat's agents share the instance's RAM — accepted deliberately; P1's memory isolation is an honest-rules reading on every runtime, and the protocol shape (no population-scoped call, single-agent observations) is unchanged.

Encoding (F3)

All observation, action, and negotiation messages are UTF-8 JSON with a versioned envelope ({v, type, ...}), schema documented in PROTOCOL.md, encoded and decoded by pure core functions shared by every shell. The rationale: zero-codegen authoring in any language, LLM-direct legibility, and eyeball debuggability. Under F1's minimal observations, messages are small — roughly 1–4 KB — and the policy boundary is not the hot loop. A binary protocol version can be added behind the same seam if profiling ever demands it. This matches every turn-based coworld in the ecosystem, including v1, and closes fork 1 of docs/what-is-a-coworld.md.

Time (A4)

The game has no notion of wall-clock time and imposes no per-invocation budget — an "invocation" being one policy call: one agent at one gate, including each negotiation-cycle delivery. A policy is either responsive or faulted (→ A3 freeze); deadlines that produce faults belong to the runtime — Arena's host per-delivery deadline, or the WS shell's per-decision timeout (config, on the order of seconds). This is forced as well as chosen: a deterministic Arena guest has no clock, so game-enforced time limits are inexpressible there, and any game-level budget would make the two runtimes play different rules. One deployment (not model) consequence: Arena runners for Sugarscape should set the per-invocation deadline to roughly 1–5 s — the 180 s host default is sized for CTF/LLM profiles — since the episode wall clock (3600 s under Arena) is the only other bound on a slow-but-legal policy. Per-policy compute fairness is the platform's job (fixed CPU per component), not the game's docs/v2-design.md §7.5.

Target semantics and the interim (2026-08-10). The desired rule is softer than freeze-forever: a missed per-decision deadline should no-op that one decision and let the seat keep playing, reserving faults for traps and protocol violations. That is currently inexpressible under Arena — no guest clock, and the host's only timeout vocabulary is the permanent drop; the limitation is described, without prescription, for the Arena team in docs/issues/arena-soft-timeout-limitation.md. Interim decision: freeze-on-timeout everywhere — both runtimes keep A3's uniform rule until Arena gains soft-timeout semantics, at which point the no-op rule switches on across both shells in the same release.

9 · Determinism Spec

  • Same config + seed + decision log ⇒ bit-identical world state on every target (F6): native today; Arena wasm32 and the browser viewer when the deferred WASM work lands.
  • No floats anywhere in the game — welfare is computed in 32.32 fixed point via pinned ln/exp implementations.
  • RNG: one master u64 seed hashed into per-subsystem PCG-class streams with explicit uint64 state.
  • A canonical per-tick state hash, recorded into replays, makes native↔wasm↔viewer parity a testable gate.

The spec has four planks docs/v2-design.md §7.7:

  1. All-integer world state. No floats anywhere in the game, including welfare: Cobb–Douglas is computed in fixed-point (32.32) via pinned polynomial/table ln/exp implementations — welfare = exp((m₁·ln w₁ + m₂·ln w₂)/mT). No libm, and no FMA/contraction discipline: v1's -ffp-contract=off era ends. Scores are exact integers, with B6's survivor bits assembled arithmetically, and results JSON carries exact integers.
  2. RNG. A master u64 seed derives per-subsystem streams — movement shuffle, lotteries, endowments, disease, child placement — by hashing (master, subsystem-id); PCG-class generators with explicit uint64 state, never platform-width int (the wasm32 32-bit trap). Per-subsystem streams keep behavior stable under feature toggles: switching disease off must not perturb the movement shuffle.
  3. Defined iteration order everywhere. Arrays and explicit sort keys; no hash-table ordering may leak into behavior.
  4. Canonical per-tick state hash, recorded periodically into the replay. The v2.0 correctness gate is live-run↔replay-resimulation hash parity on native; native↔wasm↔viewer parity joins the gate when the WASM targets land — the coworld-ctf gameHash pattern coworld-ctf/arena/.

Precision and binning (added 2026-08-10, answering the review question). 32.32 gives a lattice step of 2⁻³² ≈ 2.3×10⁻¹⁰ and ±2³¹ integer range. With integer holdings and pinned polynomial ln/exp implementations holding a few-ulp error budget, per-tick welfare carries a relative error on the order of 10⁻⁹. Binning — two different game states mapping to the same welfare value — therefore only collapses differences below ~10⁻⁹ relative, while the smallest possible real difference (one unit of holdings at plausible wealth scales) moves welfare by ~10⁻³ to 10⁻⁴ relative: about six orders of magnitude of headroom. Two notes that are spec, not commentary. First, binning never threatens determinism — same inputs produce the same bits, always; it only bounds how fine a distinction the score can express, and that bound sits far below anything gameplay produces. Second, the welfare accumulator is wider than the lattice — 96-bit (64.32) — so 1000-tick sums of large-population welfare cannot overflow or lose low bits; the published score quantizes welfare to a documented coarser step (e.g. 2⁻²⁰) with B6's survivor count occupying bits strictly below that step (12 bits covers 4096 survivors) docs/v2-design.md §7.7.

Decision — F6 (2026-08-07)

All-integer, with fixed-point welfare — not floats with discipline. Vendored-pow floating point was rejected because floats would re-enter the accumulating welfare integral and the contraction-flag discipline would return with them; deferring to a minimal spec was rejected because replay exactness remains unproven until this exact choice is made anyway. Completes fork 4 of docs/what-is-a-coworld.md.

10 · Alternatives Considered

  • Every decision below records the losing branches and the specific reason each lost — the design record in docs/v2-design.md often keeps only the winner.
  • Two entries (E1, F1) are human overrides of the analysis and are marked as such; the full override records live in the Design History appendix (§14).
  • Recurring theme: any alternative that put behavior back inside the simulation lost to P2.
  • The 2026-08-10 review round added its own rejections and supersessions — noted inline under B (rating), E4 (round caps), F1 (fixed visibility), and F2 (the scratchpad).
  • The 2026-08-11 revisions reversed two of those calls — F2's per-agent isolation (back to one instance per seat) and F4's Arena-first build order (WASM deferred) — noted inline.

A — Players and seats

A1 — total agent count. Rejected a fixed total of 64 (certification would have to simulate 64 agents; no density variants) and an agents_per_seat-only parametrization — James preferred the explicit total plus divisor validation.

A2 — newborn assignment. Rejected any single fixed rule in favor of the configurable three-mode set. Also rejected: sim-controlled children (reintroduces v1's two-tier NPC wart and violates P1/P2) and fertility-off-in-ranked (punts the question rather than answering it).

A3 — dropped seats. Rejected immediate death (infra noise would shock the ecology discontinuously for everyone else) and sim-owned fallback behavior — v1's greedy fallback generalized archived/v1/src/sugarscape/coworld.nim:467-488 — which would require the sim to implement exactly the behavior P2 evicted. Freeze wins because physics continues and no special code path exists.

A4 — budgets. Rejected game-enforced time budgets entirely: inexpressible under Arena (a deterministic guest has no clock), and any game-level budget would make the two runtimes play different rules. Time is runtime-owned.

B — Episode and scoring

B1 — length. Rejected 500 ticks (mating and lending dynamics too shallow) and 2000 ticks (v1's league value — doubles compute and squeezes the 3600 s Arena episode wall clock).

B2+B3 — wellness. Rejected all end-snapshot variants: dead-scores-0 (one death zeroes min; a loss at t=999 erases the whole episode), welfare-at-death (dying rich early scores like living rich long), and exclude-the-dead (culling your weak agents raises the reduce — monstrous). Also rejected integrating but normalizing by ticks-alive: a short spectacular life would outrank a long good one. Integration ÷ T dissolves every one of these pathologies at once.

B4 — Cobb–Douglas form. Exponents were uncontested (metabolism shares — the classic, trade-generating choice). The gross-vs-net question was deliberately deferred into E6 rather than decided early — and E6 resolved it: gross.

B5 — reduce. Rejected mean as ranked default (structurally punishes mating — late-born agents dilute the average), max (15 of a seat's 16 agents become irrelevant; invites culling-by-neglect), min (every newborn is your minimum), and median/geometric_mean (tail-blind; one near-zero agent collapses the geomean). sum was added to the table as the only reduce where survival, prosperity, and reproduction all point the same way, bounded by carrying capacity.

B6 — ties. James rejected both pure draws and full deterministic tie-break chains; chose survivors-then-draw. Implementation note: the survivor count encodes lexicographically below welfare precision in the score scalar — F6 owns the exact layout.

League rating (2026-08-10). Elo was rejected for ranked leagues: its all-pairs win/loss comparison is a poor fit for a game whose output is a continuous welfare scalar. The platform's score ranking algorithm with EWMA standing won because it fits the output directly and requires no platform modification (§3).

C — World mechanics

C1 — maps. Rejected a v1-style procedural-peaks spec (radial falloff math becomes normative spec every runtime must reproduce bit-for-bit) and supporting both formats (two validation and determinism stories). A map is data, not a program.

C2 — seasons. Rejected fractional winter growback rates (floats in the environment) and per-resource climates (no classic precedent; doubles the config surface).

C3 — pollution. Rejected observation-only pollution — provably inert under P2, since smart policies ignore costless data; the classic's pollution only worked through the hard-coded movement rule. Shipped both physical effects, independently configurable, rather than picking one story.

C4 — disease. Rejected a simpler SIR model (flatter dynamics, generic rather than specific immunity) and deferral (leaves holes in the config schema and in F1's observation contract).

C5 — resources. James rejected a first-class single-resource mode; chose always-both. Uniform schemas everywhere; sugar-only is just zero spice capacity plus zero spice metabolism, and no trade validation is needed since trade is policy-negotiated — a spiceless world has nothing to exchange.

D — Agent lifecycle

D1 — max age. Rejected ranked-immortal and no-max-age: well-played agents would live all 1000 ticks, fertility would become optional, and B5's reproduction incentive would be undermined.

D2–D4 — death and estates. Rejected inherit-else-vanish and always-vanish (deflationary; corpses inert) and always-drop (removes the dynasty investment mechanic). No-replacement was confirmed directly from P3 + P1.

D5 — genetics. Rejected all-fresh draws for newborns (mate choice becomes strategically empty — hollows E5) and a configurable genetics knob (an untested second path with no demand).

E — Actions and phases

E1 — activation. The assistant recommended simultaneous movement with a conflict lottery (fairness, latency); James overrode the recommendation and chose sequential seeded order for movement — classic semantics valued over the WS-shell latency cost, consciously accepted (and fully borne by v2.0, whose sole runtime is the WS shell since the 2026-08-11 WASM deferral). Harvest and tribe are order-free; the negotiation phases get their own structures in E4/E5.

E2 — combat. Rejected probabilistic resolution by wealth ratio (adds outcome RNG and weakens wealth-as-armor) and unrestricted attack (kamikaze griefing becomes free for doomed agents). The classic retaliation look-ahead is evicted as behavior — not dropped as an oversight.

E3 — harvest. Rejected all-or-nothing harvesting: pollution restraint would collapse to a binary choice, and C3's levers would lose their play.

E4 — trade. Rejected a sim-matched order book (the matcher re-centralizes what P2 decentralized, and bilateral strategy — partners, reputation, boycott — dissolves) and a free-form byte negotiation channel (protocol surface explodes and the binding machinery is still needed; buildable later as a variant). The 2026-08-10 review also retired the fixed 2–3 round default: caps were raised to 8 with run-to-quiescence semantics, because offer chains need enough cycles to re-propose downstream (§6).

E5 — mating. Rejected dropping the opposite-sex requirement (makes sex vestigial) and willingness-flags with sim-side pairing (partner choice disappears — hollows D5's genetic strategy).

E6 — lending. Rejected rollover-on-shortfall (zombie contracts, compounding bookkeeping) and net-of-principal welfare (belt-and-suspenders against an exploit that due ≤ T plus auto-collect already neutralize; would put principal tracking inside the scoring function). The insight worth preserving: under integrated welfare, interest is the price of welfare-time, and Cobb–Douglas concavity gains from rich→poor transfers — including E4 gifts — are legitimate distribution management under sum, not exploits.

E7 — tribes. Rejected classic cultural tags (emergent and passive — nearly invisible to a policy trying to play them; no switch gate), v1's seat-tribes (degenerates to everyone-their-own-tribe at 64 single-agent seats; zero strategic content), and deferral. Chosen allegiance is the one explicitly novel mechanic — flagged as the design's biggest untested bet (§11).

E8 — ordering. Rejected metabolism-before-actions (an agent dies before acting; breaks the classic collect-then-metabolize grace) and loan collection in phase 0 (tighter credit, lender-unfriendly).

E9 — inaction. Rejected penalizing illegal actions: a no-op is inaction, and A3's freeze rule depends on inaction being legal.

F — Policy interface and platform

F1 — observation. The assistant recommended full transparency; James overrode the recommendation and chose minimal visibility — {seat, holdings, tribe, sick} only, with max_age private to all — "poker, not chess." The consequence was flagged and accepted: mating proposals are partially blind, and voided proposals are information. Square-radius vision was also rejected — it breaks the vision = movement-range identity. Superseded (2026-08-10): the fixed minimal set gave way to per-attribute visibility config (§7), whose ranked defaults reproduce the same regime — the override's spirit survives as the default, not the rule.

F2 — scratchpad. The 2026-08-07 decision chose per-agent pads, rejecting a per-seat pad (loses per-agent replay legibility) and dual per-seat + per-agent pads (machinery without demand), with an honest-rules clause: component RAM persists per-episode, so pad scope was never an enforceable isolation boundary. Superseded (2026-08-10): the scratchpad was dropped from protocol v1 entirely — persistent instance RAM covers the memory role; what the pad offered beyond memory was not worth a protocol object. The same revision adopted per-agent component instance isolation to make P1 enforced physics under Arena; that half was reverted (2026-08-11) — one policy instance per seat, shared RAM accepted (§7). The scratchpad stays dropped.

F3 — encoding. Rejected CBOR/MessagePack (a decode tax on every debug session and every LLM policy) and a Sprite-v1-lineage binary format (built for 24 tps pixel streams — the wrong profile). The JSON family matches every turn-based coworld in the ecosystem, including v1.

F4 — runtime. The 2026-08-07 decision rejected a WS-first build order (it delays the dogfood and builds the bigger shell before the core is proven). Reversed (2026-08-11): the WASM implementation is deferred to future work, so the build order is WS-first after all — the dogfood role moves to the WS shell, and the core is still built (and proven) before any shell.

F5 — replay. Rejected scratchpad snapshots in the base replay format: bloat, and policy memory would be public by default. (Moot since 2026-08-10 — F2's revision dropped the scratchpad entirely.)

F6 — determinism. Rejected vendored-pow floats (floats re-enter the accumulating integral; the contraction discipline returns) and a minimal-spec-defer (replay exactness stays unproven until the choice is made anyway).

  • The design is complete — nothing below blocks implementation — but real risks remain, and each has a named mitigation or escape hatch.
  • The two watch-items are the two most original calls: E7's chosen-allegiance tribes and F1's hidden-by-default mating attributes.
  • Several defaults (negotiation round caps, combat α, pollution coefficients, tribe count) are placeholders pending playtesting.
Open — E7 chosen-allegiance tribes The one mechanic with no literature precedent; its dynamics are unobserved until playtesting. Fallback: the feature flag — tribeless variants gate combat on wealth alone, and ranked play can ship without tribes if the dynamics disappoint.
Open — F1 blind mating proposals Under the ranked visibility defaults a proposer cannot read a target's fertility. The risk stands, but the escape hatch got cheaper: with per-attribute visibility config (revised 2026-08-10), exposing fertile-now is a config flip, not a schema revision.
Risk — Arena scaling (largely retired 2026-08-11) The 2026-08-10 per-agent isolation made instance count track agents — a classic 250-agent world meant 251 Arena component instances, against the 17 the arena-runner experiment was sized for. The F2 re-revision retires most of this: instance count tracks seats again, so the 64-seat flagship's 65 instances is the ceiling and large total_agents configs add nothing. What remains, for the deferred Arena work (§12): 65 instances still exceeds the experiment's sizing, and per-component memory caps are host constants, not game config metta docs/specs/0076-arena-single-pod-experiment.md.
Risk — timeout semantics are interim The desired soft-timeout rule (a missed deadline no-ops one decision) is inexpressible under Arena today, so both runtimes freeze on timeout per A3 until Arena gains soft-timeout semantics (§7) — a whole seat's episode still dies to one slow decision in the interim docs/issues/arena-soft-timeout-limitation.md.
Risk — Arena is experiment-scoped (now moot for v2.0) Arena's metta stack was unmerged as of 2026-08-06 (the jb/arena-task-* branches). The 2026-08-11 WASM deferral removes the dependency entirely: v2.0 runs on the WS shell alone, and Arena's maturity becomes a question for the future-work milestone (§12), not this release.
Open — fixed-point ln/exp implementation The 32.32 ln/exp pair must be written and frozen with pinned coefficients — parity-tested on native (live run vs replay resimulation) for v2.0, and against wasm32 and the browser when the deferred WASM targets land. Until then, F6's bit-identical guarantee is a spec, not a demonstrated property.
Open — negotiation round caps trade_rounds, mating_rounds, and lend_rounds are safety caps on run-to-quiescence phases, default 8 (raised 2026-08-10 so offer chains can re-propose downstream; lend_rounds added with the lend gate, 2026-08-11); the exact value is a playtest knob trading convergence depth against per-tick policy invocations.
Open — balance unknowns Combat α (loot cap), the six pollution coefficients, and the tribe count default (K = 2) are untested balance values; playtesting owns them. None of them changes protocol shape.

12 · Future Work

  • Everything here is desired and explicitly not in v2.0.
  • The WASM implementation (deferred 2026-08-11): Arena components, the wasm32 build and its cross-target parity gates, and the browser-compiled core behind the resimulating replay viewer.
  • The other headline item is a wholly novel speaking mechanic: audible cheap talk between nearby agents, deception legal by construction, public in replays.
  • Also parked: Thue–Morse activation ordering (E1), order-book and free-form negotiation variants (E4), and an llm import for Arena player components.

The WASM implementation (deferred 2026-08-11). Everything that runs the core outside a native process: the Arena softmax:game / softmax:player components (one instance per seat, per F2's re-revision, §7), the wasm32 build and its cross-target hash-parity gates (F6), and the browser-compiled core that powers F5's resimulating replay viewer — the viewer therefore ships with this work. v2.0 is native core + WS shell; F6's all-integer discipline is maintained from day one so this lands as a port, not a redesign docs/v2-design.md §11.

Speaking (James, 2026-08-10). A wholly novel mechanic: agents speak, audibly to nearby agents — cheap talk, unverifiable, deception legal by construction, and public in replays. The design space sketched so far, to be worked when v2.0 is playable: probably targeted, E4-shaped speech rather than broadcast — an agent speaks to specific agents (ideally multiple recipients) within some region, probably neighbors rather than vision, with the phase running to quiescence like trade and mating rather than one utterance each in seeded order. Possibly speaking phases injected between all other phases — talk before you trade, before you mate, before you move — rather than one fixed slot after movement. Still open: recipient addressing, an utterance byte cap, the persistence window (this-tick vs longer), whether hearing precedes speaking within a round, and the interaction with F1's visibility regimes — speech as the channel that makes hidden-attribute inference social. One interplay worth designing for: under per-seat instances (F2, §7) an in-seat child is born into its policy's accumulated knowledge, but a cross-seat child (A2) inherits none — speech becomes the only way knowledge crosses seat lines within a family: an emergent culture channel docs/v2-design.md §11.

Also parked. A Thue–Morse activation ordering for E1 — fairness beyond per-tick reshuffle; an open exploration, not committed. Order-book matching and free-form negotiation channels as E4 variants, buildable later on the same encoding. And an llm import for Arena player components — platform-side work; the v0.1.0 wit has none.

The two override records

Decision — E1 (2026-08-07) · Human override, as originally recorded

Sequential seeded movement, by James's explicit call — overriding the recommendation. The assistant recommended simultaneous movement with a conflict lottery, on fairness and latency grounds. James chose sequential seeded order: classic Sugarscape semantics were valued above the WS-shell latency cost, and the cost was consciously accepted — N serial policy round-trips per timestep is negligible under Arena (in-process) but slow for the WS shell at full scale. The 2026-08-10 review restated the rationale on its own terms, and the body (§6) now carries it as the full-information argument: sequential activation guarantees every agent acts on exactly the world it observed — no phantom moves, no conflict lotteries — with per-tick reshuffling spreading first-action benefit and a Thue–Morse ordering parked as a candidate refinement. The call preceded the argument; the argument now stands without the override framing.

Decision — F1 (2026-08-07) · Human override, as originally recorded

Minimal visibility, by James's explicit call — overriding the recommendation. The assistant recommended full transparency of agent attributes; James chose the minimal set — {seat, holdings, tribe, sick}, with max_age private to all — "poker, not chess." The consequence was flagged and accepted: mating proposals are partially blind, and voided proposals are information. The 2026-08-10 review generalized the decision into per-attribute visibility config (§7) whose ranked defaults reproduce exactly the regime this override chose — the override's spirit survives as the default information regime rather than a fixed rule.
